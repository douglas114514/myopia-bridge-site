    Protecting young eyes

    Learn how myopia is slowed Watch interviews

    A student project · Shanghai → United States

    Myopia is not something children simply grow into.

    In 2018, eight Chinese government departments made childhood myopia a national priority. Schools now check every student's vision twice a semester and keep an eye-health file for each child. First and second graders are set no written homework, screens are capped at a share of class time, and children are sent outdoors for at least an hour a day. More than half of Chinese children are already myopic — around 80% by the end of senior high. Most American families have never been told any of this. We are three students bringing it across, in plain English, from the doctors who practise it.

    Sources: China's 2018 Implementation Plan for Comprehensive Prevention and Control of Myopia in Children and Adolescents; Community Eye Health Journal (2023); Global Eye Health Commission.

    The same curve, a few decades behind

    Our mission

    To carry proven myopia-prevention practice from China to American families — clearly, honestly, and early enough to matter for a child's eyes.

    Myopia control in China is not experimental. It is a national programme with measurement standards, school policy and a clinical routine parents understand — the plan sets a target of cutting childhood myopia by half a percentage point a year, and a full point in the worst-affected provinces. In the United States, a child's first pair of glasses is still often the end of the conversation instead of the start of one.

    Founder

    “I could do nothing but worry when my own vision began to blur.”

    I could do nothing but worry when my own vision began to blur, and that moment is still engraved in my mind. Because my mother is an ophthalmologist, eye care was familiar to me — but understanding myopia from both sides of the examination table did not make being a patient any less frustrating. I had always wanted to become a doctor; the painful contact lenses and the heavy glasses decided what kind, so that other children would not have to go through what I did.

    Eager to change that, I spent hundreds of hours volunteering in the pediatric optometry department of a general hospital in Shanghai. It was huge, yet full every day: teenagers coming alone, children and even kindergarteners with their parents, a line outside every office where patients waited at least two hours. I assisted with registration and queuing, with no time to rest. Children cried; teenagers refused to wear their glasses; parents worried that myopia would affect their child's future.

    That turned me from correction toward prevention. Myopia can now be slowed, and sometimes reduced, without surgery and without interrupting an ordinary childhood — and China has evidence behind approaches not yet proven in the United States. Meanwhile, in Florida, I watched more of my classmates put on glasses every year. So I asked leading Chinese ophthalmologists to sit down with us, and with two friends built this site and a YouTube channel to carry what they told us to American families. If one parent finds this early enough, one child will not have to learn what it is like to live behind a blur.

    Chapter 01

    What Myopia Is

    Myopia — nearsightedness — is a focusing error caused by shape. In a myopic eye, the distance from the front of the eye to the retina is too long for the eye's own focusing power. Light from far away comes to a point before it reaches the retina, so distant things blur while close things stay sharp.

    That distance has a name: axial length. It is measured in millimetres, it grows as a child grows, and in myopia it grows too fast. This is why glasses do not fix the problem — they move the focus back onto the retina, but the eye underneath keeps lengthening.

    It also explains why myopia is a health question rather than a convenience question. A longer eye is a stretched eye, and high myopia raises lifetime risk of retinal detachment, myopic maculopathy, glaucoma and early cataract. [Add the specific risk figures you want to cite, with sources.]

    [Optional paragraph: the difference between a child's myopia and an adult's, and why the years between roughly 6 and 14 are when the eye is most changeable.]

    Same eye, same lens power. The only thing that changed is length.

    Chapter 02

    Basic Eye Anatomy

    Left: Rhcastilhos and Jmarchn, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons. Right: National Eye Institute, NIH, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

    Cornea & lens

    Together they bend incoming light so it meets at a point. The cornea — the clear dome at the front — does most of the work. The lens behind the pupil fine-tunes it, changing shape to hold things sharp as they move closer.

    Iris & pupil

    The iris is the coloured ring of muscle; the pupil is the dark opening at its centre. It widens in dim rooms and narrows in daylight, which is one reason bright outdoor light affects the eye differently from indoor light.

    Retina & sclera

    The retina lines the back of the eye and turns light into signals the optic nerve carries to the brain. The sclera is the white outer wall. In myopia that wall stretches, the eye grows too long, and focus lands in front of the retina instead of on it.

    Chapter 03

    Contributing Factors

    Myopia runs in families, but family history alone cannot explain a change this fast in a single generation. Environment does much of the rest.

    Strong evidence

    Time outdoors — or the lack of it

    [The most consistent protective factor in the literature. Explain the light-intensity idea: outdoor light is 10–100× brighter than indoor lighting, and bright light appears to slow eye elongation, possibly through dopamine release in the retina. Cite the Guangzhou and Taiwan school trials.]

    Strong evidence

    Sustained near work

    [Long unbroken periods of reading, homework or screens at close distance. Distance and duration seem to matter more than the device itself. Note what is still debated.]

    Contributing

    Family history

    [Risk rises with one myopic parent and rises further with two. Genetics sets the slope; environment decides how far along it a child travels.]

    Contributing

    Early onset age

    [The younger a child becomes myopic, the more years of progression ahead of them — which is why onset age predicts final prescription better than the first prescription does.]

    Contributing

    Sleep, lighting and study load

    [Shorter sleep, dim study lighting and heavy academic schedules all appear in the research, with weaker or mixed evidence. Say so plainly — the honesty is the point.]

    Myth

    What does not cause myopia

    [Reading in the dark, sitting close to the television, carrots, and eye exercises marketed as cures. Correct these directly — parents hear them constantly.]

    Chapter 04

    Introduction to Prevention

    Two layers: habits any family can start this week, and clinical options that require an eye doctor. Habits first — they are free, and the evidence is cleanest.

    Layer one — daily habits

    1

    Two hours of daylight a day

    [Recess, walking to school, sport, anything outside. Brightness is the active ingredient, not exercise — shade on a bright day still counts.]

    2

    Break up near work

    [The 20-20-20 habit: every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds. Explain why breaks matter more than total hours.]

    3

    Keep reading distance honest

    [Roughly a forearm's length from the page. Good desk lighting. Sit up — slouching shortens the distance without anyone noticing.]

    4

    Get an eye exam on a schedule

    [How often, and what to ask for — including whether the practice measures axial length, not only refraction.]

    Layer two — clinical options

    Low-dose atropine eye drops

    [What they are, the concentrations studied, what trials show about slowing progression, and the fact that this is a prescription decision made with a doctor.]

    Orthokeratology (ortho-k)

    [Rigid lenses worn overnight that reshape the cornea. Note the fitting process and the hygiene requirements.]

    Myopia-control spectacle lenses

    [DIMS, HAL and similar designs — glasses that correct distance vision while adding peripheral defocus. Availability differs between China and the US; say where things stand.]

    Soft multifocal contact lenses

    [Daily-wear option with a similar defocus principle. Who tends to be a good candidate.]

    None of this is a prescription. Treatment choice depends on a child's age, prescription, axial length and eye health. Bring these names to an eye doctor as questions, not as decisions.
